===== ЧАСТЬ 1 =====
Two hundred thousand years ago, in southern Africa, someone carried grass into a dark cave. They tied the grass into thick bundles and laid them on soft gray ash. They made something you used last night. A bed. Not soft, but surprisingly clever. Archaeologists found what was left of it at a site called Border Cave. These are the oldest beds ever found. But think about where those people lived. No walls, no doors, no locks, no warm blankets from a store, and lions walking in the dark outside. So how did ancient humans actually sleep? Where did they lie down? Who kept watch? How many hours did they really get? The answers come from caves and ash, from tiny bits of burned plants, and from scientists who put sleep trackers on the wrists of modern hunter-gatherers. By the end of this video, you'll know why your grandma wakes at five, and why that may have saved us.

===== ЧАСТЬ 2 =====
To understand the very first human beds, we first have to climb a tree. Every evening, wild chimpanzees build a new nest. They bend branches and tuck in leaves, and finish in just a few minutes. Then they sleep high above the leopards. And they are surprisingly picky about trees. Studies show chimpanzees often choose trees with firm, springy branches for a stable bed. Our very early ancestors probably did the same. Small, light and good at climbing, they likely spent their nights in trees. But then something important began to change. Around two million years ago, Homo erectus appeared. He was tall, with long legs for walking, and he was not very good at climbing. So where did he sleep? On the ground. And the ground at night was dangerous. Hyenas, big cats and snakes hunted there. The anthropologist Richard Wrangham has a famous idea about what made ground sleep possible. Fire. Fire gave light and warmth at night, and its smoke kept some insects away. and most animals stay away from flames. In Wonderwerk Cave, in South Africa, scientists found tiny bits of ash and burned bone about one million years old, deep inside the cave, far from wind and wildfires. Not every scientist agrees on the details. Some think it happened two million years ago, others think regular fire came much later. But one thing is pretty clear. Once humans came down from the trees, they needed new ways to survive the night.

===== ЧАСТЬ 3 =====
What did a Stone Age bed look like? Let's go back to Border Cave. Archaeologist Lyn Wadley and her team studied the ancient layers under microscopes. They found grass, but not windblown grass. They found bundles of broad-leaved grass. Scientists could even identify the grass species, mostly tall, soft grasses like Guinea grass, placed on purpose near the back wall. Under the grass lay a layer of ash. Why ash? Crawling insects struggle to cross it, and it can dry them out. It worked like an early insect barrier. Now jump to seventy-seven thousand years ago, to a rock shelter called Sibudu, also in South Africa, near a river. Here, archaeologists found beds once again, made of sedges and rushes from the riverbank. Some beds covered about three square meters, big enough for a whole family. Imagine parents and kids sharing one green mattress. On top, they spread fresh leaves from a tree called river wild quince. These leaves contain chemicals that kill insects and their larvae, including hungry mosquitoes. In other words, bug-repellent mattresses, made seventy-seven thousand years ago. And there's one more clever trick. Every so often, they burned old bedding. Old grass fills up with dirt and bugs, so they burned it and cleaned the floor, then laid down a fresh new layer. Researchers found at least fifteen bedding layers, spanning about forty thousand years of sleep. Archaeologists see this pattern again and again, for tens of thousands of years. That's not just about survival anymore. That is real Stone Age housekeeping.

===== ЧАСТЬ 4 =====
Even with a good bed, nights had enemies. The first enemy was the cold. Stone Age nights had no heaters at all. On clear nights, temperatures can drop fast, even in places that are hot by day. So people slept close to the fire, close to each other, under animal hides. The second enemy was hungry predators. A sleeping human is an easy target. Leopards and hyenas hunted mostly in darkness. That's why the fire mattered so much, and why someone often fed it at night. The third enemy was the smallest one. Bugs. Fleas, lice, ticks, and more. Lice are a whole story of their own. Clothing lice could only appear after clothes. So by studying lice DNA, scientists estimated that humans were already wearing clothes about one hundred seventy thousand years ago. And then there's one pest you know. The bed bug, older than bats themselves. A 2019 study traced their long history. For ages, they fed on cave bats. Then humans moved into those same caves, and the bugs found a new meal. Human and bat bed bugs began splitting around a quarter of a million years ago. So yes, our ancestors had bed bugs too. That's why ash, fire and bug-killing leaves were so useful in a Stone Age bed.

===== ЧАСТЬ 5 =====
Okay, but how long did they actually sleep? Many of us imagine our ancestors sleeping ten or twelve hours under the stars. In 2015, Gandhi Yetish, Jerome Siegel and colleagues put that popular idea to the test. They tracked three traditional groups without electricity. First, the Hadza hunters of northern Tanzania, then the San of the Kalahari, Namibia, and the Tsimane forager-farmers of Bolivia. The results surprised a lot of people. They slept about six to seven hours. Not ten, not twelve, just six or seven. That's about the same as city people, and sometimes even a little less. But some things were very different. They didn't go to bed at sunset. They fell asleep about three hours later, as the night air slowly cooled down. They woke up when it was coldest, usually just before the sun came up. Temperature, not a clock, set their bedtime. In winter, they slept about an hour longer. Their bedtimes changed from night to night, but they woke at almost the same time. And they rarely took naps during the day. And here's the part I really love. Neither the San nor the Tsimane even have a word for insomnia. When researchers tried to explain it, the idea just didn't make sense. Meanwhile, ten to thirty percent of adults in industrial countries struggle with it.

===== ЧАСТЬ 6 =====
There's another old mystery about the night. Historian Roger Ekirch spent years in archives, reading diaries, letters and court records. He found hundreds of mentions of something called first sleep and second sleep. People went to bed soon after dark, woke after midnight for an hour or so, prayed, talked, or tended the fire, and then slept again until morning. Northern winter nights could last fourteen hours, so a break in the middle made sense. Some writers say this was our natural rhythm, and that Stone Age people slept the same. But be careful with that idea here. Ekirch's records come from recent European history, not from the Stone Age at all. And when scientists tracked the Hadza and San, they didn't find a clear two-part night. So the honest answer is this: human sleep was probably quite flexible. There was no single right way to sleep. People woke, listened, and added wood to fires, maybe whispered a little, then slept again. The night was never a wall of silence. It was full of sounds and movement, and someone was almost always listening.

===== ЧАСТЬ 7 =====
Which brings us back to your grandma. In 2017, anthropologist David Samson gave trackers to the members of a Hadza community and recorded their nights for three weeks. Then he asked a very simple question. How often was everyone asleep at once? Across twenty nights, the answer was eighteen minutes. Just eighteen minutes in total, all together. The rest of the time, someone was awake or was only sleeping very lightly. So who were these owls and early birds? The younger adults tended to stay up late. The older adults went to sleep early and woke up long before the dawn. Samson calls this the poorly sleeping grandparent hypothesis. And honestly, it may sound very familiar. Teenagers who can't fall asleep before midnight, and grandparents who are up at dawn, might be following that same old pattern. Different sleep times worked like a night watch. Nobody had to be the official guard. The group just never slept all at once. So when your grandma wakes up at five, she isn't broken or doing anything wrong. She may be running very old software, the night shift that kept our ancestors alive.

===== ЧАСТЬ 8 =====
So when did beds start looking like beds? Once people settled down and built houses, the bed finally got walls of its own. About five thousand years ago, at Skara Brae, on the Orkney islands of Scotland, every stone house had beds built into walls. Stone boxes, filled with heather or straw, around a fire in the room's center. In Egypt, rich families slept on wooden beds, and rested their heads not on pillows, but on curved headrests of wood or stone. Hard, yes, but cooler in the heat, and believed to protect the sleeper at night. From there, it was a long road to springs, foam and memory pillows. But here's the strangest part of the story. Humans sleep less than almost any other primate. We get about seven hours a night, while chimpanzees sleep around nine and a half. Why would humans sleep less than chimps? Samson and anthropologist Charles Nunn have an idea. On the ground, long and light sleep was risky, with predators all around. Shorter, deeper sleep was much safer, and it left more hours awake for making tools, sharing food and telling stories. Our sleep is also deeper and richer, with more dreaming than our closest cousins. Some scientists think that sleeping short and deep on the ground, by fire, among our group, helped make us who we are today. We traded long, lazy nights for smarter ones. So tonight, when you pull up your blanket, think of that grass bed on gray ash, made two hundred thousand years ago. And tell me in the comments below: would you trade your mattress for these leaves, and a grandma on night watch?
