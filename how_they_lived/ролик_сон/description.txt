No beds, no walls, and lions in the dark. So how did ancient humans actually sleep?
The answer comes from 200,000-year-old grass beds, bug-killing leaves, and sleep trackers on modern hunter-gatherers.

In this animated history video, we go back to Border Cave in South Africa, where archaeologists found the oldest beds ever discovered: bundles of grass laid on a layer of ash to keep crawling insects away. Then we visit Sibudu, where people made mattresses from river sedges and topped them with leaves that kill mosquitoes.

We also look at why our ancestors came down from the trees, how fire may have made sleeping on the ground possible, and how bed bugs jumped from bats to humans.

Scientists who tracked the Hadza, San and Tsimane found that people without electricity sleep about six to seven hours, fall asleep when the night gets cooler, and almost never suffer from insomnia. And among the Hadza, everyone was asleep at the same time for just eighteen minutes over twenty nights. The reason may explain why your grandma wakes up at five in the morning.
