PART 1 — THE CITY ON THE LAKE

In November 1519, a Spanish soldier named Bernal Díaz stood on a road built across a lake, and stared. In front of him, a white city seemed to float right on the water. Temples, towers, gardens, canals full of canoes. Some of his friends asked each other if what they saw was just a dream. This was Tenochtitlan, the capital of the Aztecs. Historians think a hundred to two hundred thousand people lived here at that time, more than in most cities of Europe. But let's forget the emperors and battles. What was a normal day really like for an ordinary family in this city? When did they wake up? What did they eat? Where did they go to the toilet? And why could a single cotton cloak get an ordinary man into deadly trouble? Let's spend one full day in Tenochtitlan.

PART 2 — BEFORE SUNRISE

Meet Coatl. He is not a famous person. We made him up as a typical farmer, but his day is built from real sources. He lives with his wife Citlali, their son and daughter, and his old father. Their home is a small, simple house made of sun-dried mud bricks, called adobe. Often it had just one main room. There were no beds and no chairs. Everyone slept on woven reed mats, petates, rolled out on the floor every night. Before sunrise, the temples woke the city. Priests blew conch shells and beat drums, and the sound rolled across the water. Coatl rolled up his mat and stretched. He splashed cold water on his face. And breakfast? Not yet. First came work. Most people ate just twice a day, and many worked a few hours before their first real meal of the day.

PART 3 — THE KITCHEN

Meanwhile, Citlali had the hardest job of all. Every day, she ground maize by hand on a flat stone called a metate. Historians think it could take four hours or more, though a skilled cook was faster. First, the corn was soaked with lime. This old trick made it softer, and much healthier to eat. Then she patted the dough into tortillas and cooked them on a clay griddle, a comal. With tortillas came beans, chili and squash. And a thick corn drink called atole, which almost everybody had every single day. Meat was rare for ordinary families. Turkeys and ducks were raised, but nobles were the ones who ate them often. So where did the protein come from? From the lake, and from bugs. Grasshoppers were roasted with chili, and the eggs of water flies were eaten like a kind of caviar. They even ate axolotls, the smiling salamanders that still live in Mexico City today. And from the lake surface, they scooped green algae and dried it into cakes. Spaniards compared it to a kind of cheese. Today we sell it as spirulina. And dogs? Yes, small dogs were raised for food too. Sorry, dog lovers.

PART 4 — FARMS ON THE WATER

After the morning work, Coatl took his canoe out to his fields. And his fields were built right in the lake. They were called chinampas. Farmers piled up mud and plants from the bottom of the shallow lake, and planted willow trees around the edges so their roots held everything together. The soil was so rich that some fields may have given up to seven harvests a year. These gardens helped feed a huge city. Coatl grew maize, beans, squash, peppers, and flowers too, because the Aztecs loved flowers. Some chinampas are still farmed today, in Xochimilco, at the edge of Mexico City.

PART 5 — THE CLEANEST CITY

Now, here's something that shocked the Spaniards. The Aztecs were very, very clean. Many of them bathed every single day. Emperor Moctezuma reportedly bathed twice a day. For soap, they used plants. The fruit of the soap tree made a rich foam for hair and clothes. They also loved steam baths called temazcales. And the streets? Every day, around a thousand workers swept and watered them. Meanwhile, in many European cities, people still dumped waste in the street. So where did the Aztecs go to the toilet? There were public toilets along the roads, on the causeways, and even in the market. And nothing went to waste. Literally. Workers collected it by canoe and used it to fertilize the fields, or sold it for tanning animal hides. Even urine was saved in clay pots to help fix colors when dyeing cloth. And clean drinking water? It came from springs on the mainland, through an aqueduct. The lake itself was salty in places, so clean water was a very big deal.

PART 6 — THE GREAT MARKET

Around midday, Citlali headed to the market. Not just any market. The biggest in the Americas. It was in Tlatelolco, the city's twin. Cortés wrote that over sixty thousand people came there to buy and sell every day. You could find almost anything here. Fruit, vegetables, turkeys, rabbits, cloth, pottery, feathers, knives made of obsidian, medicine, firewood, even ready-to-eat food. Each kind of goods had its own section, and judges walked around checking prices and settling arguments on the spot. But how did you pay without coins? With chocolate. Well, with cacao beans. Small things were paid for in beans, big deals in lengths of cotton cloth. A document from 1545 lists some prices. A big tomato cost one bean. A rabbit cost thirty beans, and a turkey hen cost one hundred. And since money grew on trees, some people faked it. They emptied real beans and filled them with dirt, or shaped fake beans out of clay or wax. Counterfeit chocolate money, five hundred years ago.

PART 7 — SCHOOL FOR EVERYONE

While the adults worked, what about the kids? Until about fourteen, they learned at home. Boys learned their father's work, girls learned to cook, spin and weave. Parents gave long speeches full of advice about being polite, modest and hardworking. And here's a surprise. School was for everyone. Every boy, rich or poor, had to go, at a time when most kids in Europe never went to school at all. Common boys went to the house of youth, where they trained as warriors and workers. Noble boys went to a stricter school to become priests, judges and leaders. And many teens learned songs and dances in special houses of song. But Aztec parents could be very strict. A colonial book called the Codex Mendoza shows a punishment for naughty eleven-year-olds: holding them over the smoke of burning chili peppers. Nobody knows how long it lasted, but most kids probably behaved after that.

PART 8 — RULES FOR EVERYTHING

Aztec life had rules for almost everything, even for your clothes. Ordinary people wore cloth made from maguey, a tough fiber from the agave plant. Cotton was for the nobles. A commoner's cloak had to end at the knee. Only nobles wore them down to the ankles. Break that rule, and the punishment could be death. Fashion was serious business. Alcohol had rules too. The Aztecs brewed pulque from agave sap, a milky, slightly sour drink. But public drunkenness was a serious crime. A drunk could be shamed in public, and repeat offenders could even be executed. But there was one exception: the elderly. Old people had earned the right to drink at festivals without punishment. Getting old finally had its perks.

PART 9 — FUN AND GAMES

But life wasn't only work and rules. The Aztecs loved games. A favorite was patolli, a board game played with beans instead of dice. And people bet on it. A lot. Cloaks, jewels, food, and some gamblers even lost their homes or their freedom. Then there was the famous ball game. Players hit a heavy rubber ball with their hips, not their hands. Scoring through the stone ring was rare, and it could end the game at once. The rubber came from the sap of trees, long before Europeans had ever seen rubber. Yes, the game also had a religious side, like almost everything in Aztec life. Their religion did include human sacrifice, and historians still argue about the numbers. But for most families, on most days, life was about food, family and work.

PART 10 — THE NIGHT THE WORLD COULD END

As the sun went down, the city slowed. Families ate their second meal, told stories, and went to sleep early. Fires burned on the temples all night. Priests kept watch and studied the stars. Because time was a big deal for the Aztecs. They used two calendars at the same time, one of 365 days, and one of 260. And every fifty-two years, both cycles ended on the very same day. The Aztecs feared the world might end then. So on that night, every fire was put out, from the great temples to every kitchen. People threw away old pots and swept their homes. Then everyone looked toward a hill, where priests tried to light a new fire. If the fire caught, the world would continue for another fifty-two years. Runners carried the flame to every home. The last New Fire Ceremony was in 1507. Nobody knew it would be the last one.

PART 11 — THE END OF THEIR WORLD

Twelve years later, the Spaniards arrived. By 1521, after war and smallpox, Tenochtitlan had fallen. The Spaniards built Mexico City on top of it. But the Aztec world never fully disappeared. Over a million people still speak Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs. And you use their words every day. Chocolate. Tomato. Avocado. Chili. Coyote. All of them come from Nahuatl. So, could you live like Coatl? Up before dawn, no breakfast, school for everyone, a daily bath, bugs for protein, and strict rules about your cloak? Tell me in the comments.
